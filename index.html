English Literature  ·  Elizabethan Drama

The Tragedy of
Doctor Faustus

by Christopher Marlowe

A brilliant scholar sells his soul to the Devil for 24 years of power — and then has to pay the price.

Good morning everyone. Today I am going to talk about Doctor Faustus, a famous English play by Christopher Marlowe. It is the story of a man who makes a deal with the Devil. Let's see why he does it and what happens to him.

The Writer & the Play

Christopher Marlowe (1564 – 1593)

Born in Canterbury in the same year as Shakespeare. He studied at Cambridge and became one of the most exciting playwrights in London.

  • Other plays: Tamburlaine, The Jew of Malta, Edward II
  • Ben Jonson praised "Marlowe's mighty line"
  • People said he was an atheist and possibly a government spy
  • Killed in Deptford, London, stabbed above the eye at only 29
Writtenc. 1588 – 1592
On stagefirst record 1594, the Rose Theatre
Printed1604 (A-text), 1616 (B-text)
SourceGerman legend of a real magician, Johann Faust
SettingWittenberg, Rome, the Emperor's court
Marlowe was born in 1564, the same year as Shakespeare. He had a short and dangerous life, and he died in a fight when he was only 29. He wrote Doctor Faustus around 1590. The story comes from a German legend about a real magician called Johann Faust.

Act 1 · The Choice

"I have studied everything... and it is not enough"

In his study, Faustus looks at every university subject and rejects them one by one.

Logic

"Is, to dispute well, logic's chiefest end?" He can already argue well.

Medicine

Whole cities escaped the plague thanks to him, but he cannot make men live forever.

Law

"This study fits a mercenary drudge." Only good for money.

Theology

"The reward of sin is death." He stops reading before the promise of God's forgiveness.

Magic

"A sound magician is a mighty god." He chooses power.

He reads only half of each Bible verse, so he misses the message of mercy. This is his first self-deception.

At the start, Faustus is bored with normal knowledge. Look at theology: he reads only half of the Bible verse. The next part says God gives eternal life and forgives. He does not see it. So he chooses magic, because for him magic means power.

A contract signed in blood

2 · The priceHe gives "both body and soul" to Lucifer
→
3 · BloodHe cuts his arm, but his blood congeals — a warning
→
4 · "Homo, fuge""Man, fly!" appears on his arm
→
5 · SignedDevils bring crowns and rich clothes to delight him
Consummatum est. "It is finished" — Faustus, after signing. These are Christ's last words on the cross.

Why does it matter?

Even his own body tries to stop him. Faustus is not tricked — he chooses with open eyes.

This is the key scene. Faustus gets 24 years of power, and he gives his body and soul to Lucifer. When he cuts his arm, his blood stops flowing. Then the Latin words "Homo, fuge", which mean "man, fly", appear on his arm. He sees all these warnings, but he signs anyway.
05 / 09
Mephistopheles

An honest devil

Why, this is hell, nor am I out of it. Mephistopheles
Hell hath no limits, nor is circumscrib'd
In one self place; for where we are is hell. Mephistopheles

What does he mean?

For Mephistopheles, hell is not only a place of fire. Hell is being far from God.

He even says: "O, Faustus, leave these frivolous demands." But Faustus answers: "I think hell's a fable."

Irony: the devil tells the truth, and the human does not listen.

Mephistopheles is not a typical monster. He is sad and honest. He says he is always in hell, because he has lost God. He even warns Faustus to stop. But Faustus thinks hell is just a story. This is a great example of dramatic irony.

The 24 Years

Great power... for small tricks

What he dreamed of

To be "great emperor of the world" and to "wall all Germany with brass".

→

What he actually did

Played jokes on the Pope, entertained an emperor, and tricked a horse dealer.

The SinsLucifer shows him the Seven Deadly Sins like a show
RomeInvisible, he snatches the Pope's food and hits him
The EmperorHe raises the spirit of Alexander the Great
The Horse-courserHe sells a horse that turns into a bundle of hay
Helen of Troy"Sweet Helen, make me immortal with a kiss."
This is the sad irony of the play. Faustus wanted to rule the world. But with his power he only does small, silly things: jokes, magic shows and tricks. At the end he calls Helen of Troy and asks her for a kiss to make him immortal. He wastes his 24 years.
07 / 09
The Final Hour

Eleven o'clock... midnight

The stars move still, time runs, the clock will strike,
The devil will come, and Faustus must be damn'd.Faustus, final soliloquy
  • He asks the heavens to stop so that midnight never comes
  • "See, see, where Christ's blood streams in the firmament!"
  • He wants the earth to open, or his soul to become "little water-drops"
  • His last words: "I'll burn my books! Ah, Mephistophilis!" Then the devils take him.
  • In the 1616 text, his friends find "Faustus' limbs, / All torn asunder" the next morning.
In the last scene Faustus is alone, with only one hour to live. He begs time to stop. He sees Christ's blood in the sky, but he feels he cannot reach it. He wants to hide, and he even promises to burn his books. But it is too late. At midnight the devils take him away. In the longer 1616 version, his friends find his body torn to pieces the next morning.

Themes

The big ideas

Ambition & pride

Faustus wants to be more than a human. His pride, his tragic flaw, destroys him.

Knowledge vs. power

He says he wants knowledge, but he really wants power, pleasure and fame.

Sin & repentance

The Good Angel and the Old Man tell him to repent. He never truly does.

Free will

Is he a victim of fate, or a man who makes his own choices? The play leaves this open.

His waxen wings did mount above his reach, And, melting, heavens conspir'd his overthrow.The Chorus, Prologue — Faustus is compared to Icarus
There are four main themes: ambition, knowledge and power, sin and repentance, and free will. At the beginning, the Chorus compares Faustus to Icarus, the boy in Greek mythology who flew too close to the sun with wings made of wax. Too much ambition can destroy you.

Conclusion

Why Faustus still matters

Cut is the branch that might have grown full straight,
And burned is Apollo's laurel-bough.The Chorus, Epilogue — a great talent, wasted

A "Faustian bargain"

Today this English expression means a deal where you get what you want now, but lose something much more important later.

The story lives on, for example in Goethe's Faust (Part One, 1808).

Thank you!  Any questions?

At the end, the Chorus says Faustus was like a branch that could grow straight, but it was cut. He had great talent, but he wasted it. Today we still use the phrase "Faustian bargain". So the question for all of us is: what would you give for success? Thank you for listening. Any questions?
